Exchange Rates and Foreign Exchange Markets

How do exchange rates affect international transactions, and what links exchange rates to interest rates and prices?

This note accompanies Lectures 16–17 and the relevant material in Feenstra–Taylor Chapters 13–14. The goal is to understand the equations and their economic intuition, not simply memorize them.

1. Exchange Rate Quotations and Cross Rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. In ECON 3200, we quote it as the domestic currency price of one unit of foreign currency. For the United States and Eurozone,

\[ E_{\$/€}=1.12\ \frac{\$}{€}. \]

One euro costs $1.12. The reciprocal quote is

\[ E_{€/\$}=\frac{1}{E_{\$/€}}=\frac{1}{1.12}=0.893\ \frac{€}{\$}. \]

Always check the units. For example, a product priced at €50 costs $56 at this exchange rate: \(50\,\mathrm{EUR}\times 1.12\,\mathrm{USD/EUR}=56\,\mathrm{USD}\).

With our convention:

  • If \(E_{\$/€}\) increases, one euro costs more dollars: the dollar depreciates against the euro.
  • If \(E_{\$/€}\) decreases, one euro costs fewer dollars: the dollar appreciates against the euro.

The percentage change is

\[ \%\Delta E_{\$/€} =100\times\frac{E_{\mathrm{new}}-E_{\mathrm{old}}}{E_{\mathrm{old}}}. \]

For example, an increase from $1.10/€ to $1.21/€ is a 10% increase in the dollar-per-euro exchange rate, indicating dollar depreciation. Percentage changes in a rate and its reciprocal are not exactly equal in magnitude.

Cross rates allow us to calculate the exchange rate between two currencies using their dollar exchange rates. Suppose

\[ E_{\$/€}=1.20\ \frac{\$}{€}, \qquad E_{\$/\mathrm{C}\$}=0.80\ \frac{\$}{\mathrm{C}\$}. \]

The Canadian-dollar price of one euro is

\[ E_{\mathrm{C}\$/€} =\frac{E_{\$/€}}{E_{\$/\mathrm{C}\$}} =\frac{1.20\,\$/€}{0.80\,\$/\mathrm{C}\$} =1.50\ \frac{\mathrm{C}\$}{€}. \]

The dollar units cancel. This formula follows from triangular arbitrage: converting euros directly into Canadian dollars must give the same amount as converting euros into U.S. dollars and then into Canadian dollars, apart from transaction costs.

2. Effective Exchange Rates and Exchange Rate Regimes

A currency may appreciate against one trading partner and depreciate against another. A nominal effective exchange rate summarizes bilateral movements using trade weights:

\[ \%\Delta E_{\mathrm{effective}} \approx\sum_j w_j\,\%\Delta E_{\$/j}, \qquad \sum_j w_j=1. \]

For example, if 60% of trade is with the Eurozone and 40% with Canada, and the dollar-per-foreign-currency rates change by \(+5\%\) and \(-2\%\), respectively,

\[ \%\Delta E_{\mathrm{effective}} \approx 0.60(5\%)+0.40(-2\%)=2.2\%. \]

Under this dollar-per-foreign-currency definition, an increase means dollar depreciation against the trade-weighted basket. Some published dollar indexes use the inverse quotation, so check the index definition before interpreting a graph.

Exchange rate behavior also depends on the exchange rate regime:

  • Floating: the exchange rate is primarily market-determined.
  • Pegged or fixed: policymakers seek to keep the exchange rate at or near a target, sometimes within a band.
  • Crawling peg: the target exchange rate is adjusted gradually over time.

A nearly flat exchange-rate series may indicate a peg, whereas a steady trend may indicate a crawling arrangement. A sudden discrete change in an official peg is called a devaluation or revaluation, depending on direction. A graph alone does not always reveal the full policy arrangement.

3. Spot, Forward, and Covered Interest Parity

In the spot market, currencies are exchanged at the current spot rate \(E_{\$/€}\). A forward contract fixes today the rate \(F_{\$/€}\) for an exchange at a specified future date. Forwards can hedge exchange-rate risk.

For example, a U.S. exporter expecting a euro payment in three months can arrange today to sell euros forward for dollars. This fixes the future dollar revenue regardless of the spot rate three months later, but also gives up any benefit from a favorable exchange-rate movement.

Now suppose an investor has $1 today and can invest for one year. Let \(i_\$\) and \(i_€\) be the one-year dollar and euro interest rates. The investor has two alternatives:

Strategy Dollar payoff in one year
Invest directly in a U.S. dollar deposit \(1+i_\$\)
Buy euros at spot, invest in euros, and sell the future euros forward \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{E_{\$/€}}(1+i_€)F_{\$/€}\)

If both investments have the same risk and there are no relevant trading frictions, covered interest parity (CIP) requires equal payoffs:

\[ \boxed{ 1+i_\$ =\frac{1}{E_{\$/€}}(1+i_€)F_{\$/€} } \]

Rearranging,

\[ \boxed{ F_{\$/€} =E_{\$/€}\frac{1+i_\$}{1+i_€} } \]

The forward premium is therefore

\[ \frac{F_{\$/€}-E_{\$/€}}{E_{\$/€}} =\frac{i_\$-i_€}{1+i_€} \approx i_\$-i_€. \]

This approximation works well for relatively small interest rates. All interest rates must match the maturity of the forward contract: for example, use three-month returns with a three-month forward rate.

Why does CIP hold? If the dollar deposit offers the higher covered return, traders can borrow euros, convert them into dollars at spot, invest the dollars, and buy euros forward to repay the euro loan. If the covered euro deposit offers the higher return, traders can borrow dollars, buy and invest euros, and sell euros forward. These trades tend to eliminate the difference in covered returns.

A frequent mistake is to conclude that \(F_{\$/€}>E_{\$/€}\) guarantees a profit. It does not. The forward rate must be compared with the interest-rate-adjusted spot rate in the CIP equation.

4. Uncovered Interest Parity and Expectations

What if the investor buys euros and earns euro interest without selling them forward? The dollar return now depends on the unknown future spot exchange rate.

Write \(E_{\$/€}^{e}\) for the expected future dollar-per-euro exchange rate over the same investment horizon. The expected dollar payoff on the euro strategy is

\[ \frac{1}{E_{\$/€}}(1+i_€)E_{\$/€}^{e}. \]

If investors are risk-neutral and the two deposits are otherwise comparable, uncovered interest parity (UIP) implies

\[ \boxed{ 1+i_\$ =\frac{1}{E_{\$/€}}(1+i_€)E_{\$/€}^{e} } \]

Equivalently,

\[ \frac{E_{\$/€}^{e}}{E_{\$/€}} =\frac{1+i_\$}{1+i_€}. \]

To interpret this result, use the identity

\[ \frac{E_{\$/€}^{e}}{E_{\$/€}} =1+\frac{E_{\$/€}^{e}-E_{\$/€}}{E_{\$/€}}. \]

Substituting into UIP and ignoring the product of two small rates gives

\[ \boxed{ \frac{E_{\$/€}^{e}-E_{\$/€}}{E_{\$/€}} \approx i_\$-i_€ } \]

Thus, the expected rate of dollar depreciation is approximately the dollar-minus-euro interest-rate differential. If the dollar interest rate is higher, UIP predicts that the dollar is expected to depreciate, offsetting the extra interest earned on dollar deposits.

CIP uses the known forward rate \(F_{\$/€}\) and compares covered, approximately riskless returns. UIP uses the expected future spot rate \(E_{\$/€}^{e}\) and compares risky expected returns. If both conditions hold, then

\[ F_{\$/€}=E_{\$/€}^{e}. \]

But this equality is not guaranteed: risk aversion, risk premiums, and market frictions may cause UIP to fail even when CIP holds closely. Nor does UIP predict that the actual future spot rate must equal its expectation.

5. The Law of One Price, PPP, and Real Exchange Rates

Exchange rates convert prices into a common currency. For an individual good \(g\), define

\[ q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}^g =\frac{E_{\$/€}P_{\mathrm{EUR}}^g}{P_{\mathrm{US}}^g}, \]

where \(P_{\mathrm{EUR}}^g\) is the price in euros and \(P_{\mathrm{US}}^g\) is the price in dollars.

  • If \(q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}^g<1\), the good is cheaper in Europe than in the United States, measured in dollars.
  • If \(q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}^g>1\), the good is more expensive in Europe than in the United States.

For an identical, freely traded good with negligible transaction costs, traders can buy where the price is low and sell where it is high. The law of one price (LOOP) then predicts

\[ P_{\mathrm{US}}^g=E_{\$/€}P_{\mathrm{EUR}}^g \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}^g=1. \]

LOOP may fail when transportation costs, tariffs, taxes, regulations, or other barriers prevent profitable arbitrage. It is especially unlikely to hold for many services, such as haircuts and restaurant meals.

Now replace the price of one good with the price of a comparable basket of goods and services. Let \(P_{\mathrm{US}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{EUR}}\) denote aggregate price levels. The real exchange rate (RER) is

\[ \boxed{ q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}} =\frac{E_{\$/€}P_{\mathrm{EUR}}}{P_{\mathrm{US}}} } \]

It is unit-free and measures the dollar price of the European basket relative to the U.S. basket.

  • \(q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}>1\): the European basket is relatively expensive; the dollar is weak (undervalued) relative to the PPP benchmark.
  • \(q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}<1\): the European basket is relatively cheap; the dollar is strong (overvalued) relative to the PPP benchmark.
  • \(q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}=1\): the baskets cost the same in a common currency; absolute purchasing power parity (PPP) holds.

Under absolute PPP,

\[ \boxed{ E_{\$/€}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{US}}}{P_{\mathrm{EUR}}} } \]

For instance, if comparable baskets cost $120 and €100, the PPP-implied rate is $1.20 per euro. This is a benchmark, not a claim that the observed exchange rate must equal $1.20.

An increase in \(q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}\) is a real depreciation of the dollar. A decrease is a real appreciation. Nominal and real appreciation need not move in the same direction, because domestic and foreign price levels can also change.

6. Absolute and Relative PPP

From the definition of the real exchange rate,

\[ q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}} =\frac{E_{\$/€}P_{\mathrm{EUR}}}{P_{\mathrm{US}}}, \]

small percentage changes satisfy approximately

\[ \%\Delta q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}} \approx\%\Delta E_{\$/€} +\pi_{\mathrm{EUR}}-\pi_{\mathrm{US}}, \]

where \(\pi_{\mathrm{US}}\) and \(\pi_{\mathrm{EUR}}\) are the inflation rates over the same period.

Relative PPP says that the real exchange rate remains constant, not necessarily equal to one. Setting \(\%\Delta q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}=0\) gives

\[ \boxed{ \%\Delta E_{\$/€} \approx\pi_{\mathrm{US}}-\pi_{\mathrm{EUR}} } \]

If U.S. inflation is 5% and Eurozone inflation is 2%, relative PPP predicts approximately 3% nominal dollar depreciation against the euro.

The distinction is important:

  • Absolute PPP: \(q=1\) — equal price levels in a common currency.
  • Relative PPP: \(\%\Delta q=0\) — the relative price levels are stable, even if \(q\ne1\).

Absolute PPP is not a good description of international price levels: many goods and services cannot be traded or arbitraged, and poorer countries often have lower common-currency price levels. Relative PPP is also a poor guide to short-run exchange-rate fluctuations, but has more empirical support over long periods. The Big Mac Index illustrates the price comparison behind PPP, while also highlighting why a single good is an imperfect measure of an economy’s overall price level.

7. Practice Problems

Practice 1: Quotations, Cross Rates, and Effective Exchange Rates

In Year 1, suppose \(E_{\$/€}=1.10\) and \(E_{\$/\mathrm{C}\$}=0.80\). In Year 2, these rates are 1.155 and 0.784. Assume the Eurozone and Canada account for 60% and 40% of U.S. trade, respectively.

  1. In Year 1, how many Canadian dollars does one euro buy? Show the currency units in your calculation.
  2. Calculate the percentage change in each dollar-per-foreign-currency exchange rate. Did the dollar appreciate or depreciate against each currency?
  3. Calculate the approximate change in the effective exchange rate, using the trade weights above. Interpret the sign.

Practice 2: Hedging an Export Payment

A U.S. exporter expects to receive C$100,000 in three months. The three-month forward rate is $0.75 per Canadian dollar. The spot rate in three months may be either $0.70 or $0.82 per Canadian dollar.

  1. How many U.S. dollars will the exporter receive under each future spot rate without hedging?
  2. What forward transaction can the exporter use to eliminate this uncertainty?
  3. How many dollars will the exporter receive with a forward hedge? What potential benefit does the exporter give up?

Practice 3: Covered Interest Parity and Arbitrage

Suppose the one-year rates are \(i_\$=4\%\) and \(i_€=2\%\), and the spot exchange rate is \(E_{\$/€}=1.10\).

  1. Calculate the forward rate implied by CIP.
  2. Suppose the market instead quotes \(F_{\$/€}=1.14\). Calculate the covered dollar return on euro deposits and compare it with the dollar-deposit return. Describe a trading strategy that exploits the difference, including which currency to borrow and which currency to sell forward.
  3. Repeat the direction-of-trade reasoning if the market forward rate is instead \(F_{\$/€}=1.10\).
  4. Why is \(F_{\$/€}>E_{\$/€}\) alone insufficient to establish an arbitrage opportunity?

Practice 4: Uncovered Interest Parity

Suppose \(E_{\$/€}=1.10\), \(i_\$=5\%\), and \(i_€=2\%\), with all rates referring to one year.

  1. Under UIP, calculate the expected exchange rate one year from now.
  2. What is the exact implied expected percentage change in \(E_{\$/€}\)? Compare it with the interest differential approximation.
  3. If CIP also holds, what is the equilibrium one-year forward rate?
  4. Does UIP guarantee that the actual exchange rate in one year will equal your answer to (1)? Explain.

Practice 5: Goods Prices, the RER, and Relative PPP

An identical good sells for $150 in the United States and €110 in Europe. The exchange rate is $1.20 per euro. Separately, suppose the price of a comparable consumption basket is $125 in the United States and €100 in Europe, at that same exchange rate.

  1. Calculate the good-level relative price \(q_{\mathrm{US/EUR}}^g\). Assuming costless trade, where would an arbitrageur buy the good and where would they sell it? What pressures would this create on the two local prices?
  2. Calculate the basket-level real exchange rate. Does absolute PPP hold? Is the dollar overvalued or undervalued relative to the PPP benchmark?
  3. Now suppose U.S. inflation is 4%, Eurozone inflation is 2%, and the nominal dollar-per-euro exchange rate increases by approximately 2% over the next year. What does the real-exchange-rate change formula predict?
  4. Can relative PPP hold even if absolute PPP does not? Use the preceding calculations to explain.
