Immigration: Employment, Wages, and Factor Returns

How can immigration increase production while making some existing residents worse off?

We extend the specific-factors model from the previous note. Manufacturing uses capital and labor; agriculture uses land and labor. Capital and land remain fixed in their respective sectors, while labor moves freely between sectors. Throughout the baseline analysis, goods prices, technology, and the supplies of specific factors are fixed. Immigrants and existing workers are perfect substitutes, and each supplies one unit of labor.

1. Immigration, Employment, and Wages

Start from the familiar equilibrium conditions:

\[ W=P_M MPL_M=P_A MPL_A, \qquad L_M+L_A=L. \]

At the fixed supplies of capital and land, use

\[ Q_M=50L_M^{0.5},\qquad Q_A=90L_A^{0.5}. \]

Differentiating gives the marginal products of labor:

\[ MPL_M=25L_M^{-0.5},\qquad MPL_A=45L_A^{-0.5}. \]

Equal wages across sectors imply

\[ 25P_M L_M^{-0.5}=45P_A L_A^{-0.5} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{L_M}{L_A}=\left(\frac{5P_M}{9P_A}\right)^2. \]

Combining this ratio with full employment gives

\[ \boxed{ L_M=\frac{(25P_M)^2}{(25P_M)^2+(45P_A)^2}L, \qquad L_A=\frac{(45P_A)^2}{(25P_M)^2+(45P_A)^2}L. } \]

Immigration expands employment in both sectors. In this example, fixed prices also imply fixed employment shares: the two sectors expand proportionally. This proportional expansion depends on the common exponent of \(0.5\); it is not a general property of every specific-factors model.

To solve for the wage, rearrange each sector’s labor-demand condition:

\[ L_M=\left(\frac{25P_M}{W}\right)^2, \qquad L_A=\left(\frac{45P_A}{W}\right)^2. \]

Adding them and solving for \(W\) yields

\[ L=\frac{(25P_M)^2+(45P_A)^2}{W^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{W=\left[\frac{(25P_M)^2+(45P_A)^2}{L}\right]^{0.5}.} \]

Suppose immigration increases labor from \(L_0\) to \(L_1=L_0+\Delta L\). Then

\[ \frac{L_{M1}}{L_{M0}}=\frac{L_{A1}}{L_{A0}}=\frac{L_1}{L_0}, \qquad \boxed{\frac{W_1}{W_0}=\left(\frac{L_1}{L_0}\right)^{-0.5}<1.} \]

With more workers and unchanged capital and land, labor’s marginal product falls. Because goods prices are fixed, both real wages, \(W/P_M\) and \(W/P_A\), also fall.

Manufacturing employment is measured from the left origin. Agricultural employment is measured leftward from the relevant right origin. Immigration moves that right origin from \(L_0\) to \(L_1\), shifting the agricultural value-of-marginal-product curve to the right. The manufacturing curve stays fixed. Equilibrium moves from A to B: the wage falls and employment rises in both sectors.

2. Output and Returns to Specific Factors

Substitute the change in employment into the production functions:

\[ \frac{Q_{M1}}{Q_{M0}} =\left(\frac{L_{M1}}{L_{M0}}\right)^{0.5} =\left(\frac{L_1}{L_0}\right)^{0.5}, \]

\[ \frac{Q_{A1}}{Q_{A0}} =\left(\frac{L_{A1}}{L_{A0}}\right)^{0.5} =\left(\frac{L_1}{L_0}\right)^{0.5}. \]

Both outputs rise, even though each worker produces less at the margin.

The total income accruing to each sector’s specific factor equals revenue minus the wage bill:

\[ I_K=P_MQ_M-WL_M, \qquad I_T=P_AQ_A-WL_A. \]

These are payments to capital and land, respectively. To see why they rise, differentiate capital income with respect to the economy’s labor supply, holding prices, technology, and specific-factor supplies fixed:

\[ \frac{dI_K}{dL} =P_M MPL_M\frac{dL_M}{dL} -W\frac{dL_M}{dL} -L_M\frac{dW}{dL}. \]

The first two terms cancel because \(P_M MPL_M=W\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac{dI_K}{dL}=-L_M\frac{dW}{dL}>0.} \]

The same argument applies to land:

\[ \boxed{\frac{dI_T}{dL}=-L_A\frac{dW}{dL}>0.} \]

The additional worker’s revenue contribution equals their wage at the margin. The lower equilibrium wage raises the residual income going to the specific factor. Since the supplies of capital and land are fixed, their rental payments per unit also rise.

A numerical example. Let \(P_M=9\), \(P_A=5\), and initially \(L_0=50\). Immigration adds \(22\) workers, so \(L_1=72\). Wage equality gives \(L_M=L_A\), and

\[ W_0=225(25)^{-0.5}=45, \qquad W_1=225(36)^{-0.5}=37.5. \]

Variable Before immigration After immigration
Manufacturing employment, \(L_M\) 25 36
Agricultural employment, \(L_A\) 25 36
Wage, \(W\) 45 37.5
Manufacturing output, \(Q_M\) 250 300
Agricultural output, \(Q_A\) 450 540
Capital income, \(I_K\) 1,125 1,350
Land income, \(I_T\) 1,125 1,350

For example, after immigration,

\[ I_{K1}=9(300)-37.5(36)=1{,}350, \qquad I_{T1}=5(540)-37.5(36)=1{,}350. \]

The original \(50\) workers’ total labor income falls from \(45(50)=2{,}250\) to \(37.5(50)=1{,}875\). Meanwhile, the wage bill for all workers rises to \(37.5(72)=2{,}700\). A larger wage bill does not mean that each worker earns more.

Immigration therefore has distributional effects: existing residents who rely only on labor income lose, while owners of capital and land gain. For residents receiving both labor and specific-factor income, the net effect depends on their income mix.

3. What Does the Model Predict in the Data?

The model compares an economy with immigration to the same economy without immigration, holding other determinants of labor demand fixed. It does not say that wages must fall over calendar time whenever immigrants arrive.

For example, productivity growth can raise wages while immigration pushes them below the level they would otherwise have reached. An observed wage increase can therefore coexist with a negative effect of immigration relative to the no-immigration counterfactual.

The Mariel Boatlift evidence discussed in Lecture 11 illustrates the empirical question: incumbent low-skilled wages did not show the decline suggested by the simplest fixed-demand prediction. Interpreting this evidence requires examining the model’s assumptions. How substitutable are new and existing workers? How elastic is labor demand? Do existing residents move to other labor markets? Have capital, technology, or goods prices changed?

Within our baseline, identical workers earn the same wage. Explaining different wage responses across worker groups requires extending the homogeneous-labor assumption. Similarly, allowing capital to adjust changes the fixed-specific-factor comparison developed above.

4. Practice Problems

Practice 1: Immigration with Unequal Sector Sizes

Use the same production functions:

\[ Q_M=50L_M^{0.5},\qquad Q_A=90L_A^{0.5}. \]

Let \(P_M=2\), \(P_A=1\), and initially \(L_0=181\). Immigration adds \(44\) workers, so \(L_1=225\). Prices, technology, capital, and land remain fixed.

  1. Derive the equilibrium ratio \(L_M/L_A\). Calculate employment in each sector and the wage before and after immigration. Report numerical answers to two decimal places.
  2. Calculate both outputs and the total incomes accruing to capital and land before and after immigration.
  3. Compare the original workforce’s total labor income, \(WL_0\), with the wage bill for all workers, \(WL\). How does each change after immigration?
  4. Explain why aggregate employment expands in both sectors. Does the model identify which sector each immigrant works in?

Practice 2: Wage Growth and the Counterfactual

Start from the same baseline production functions, with any positive initial labor supply \(L_0\) and fixed goods prices. Between the initial and final periods, labor supply increases by \(21\%\) through immigration, and productivity rises by \(10\%\) in both sectors. The new production functions are

\[ \widetilde Q_M=1.10(50L_M^{0.5}), \qquad \widetilde Q_A=1.10(90L_A^{0.5}). \]

Capital and land remain fixed. Compare each scenario below with the same initial equilibrium.

  1. If only immigration occurred, what would be the percentage change in the wage?
  2. If only productivity improved, what would be the percentage change in the wage?
  3. Derive the wage change when both occur. Does the equilibrium allocation of labor across sectors change in proportional terms?
  4. Compare the final wage with the counterfactual wage under productivity growth without immigration. Express the difference as a percentage of that counterfactual wage.
  5. Explain why observing no wage decline over time is not enough to conclude that immigration had no effect on wages.
