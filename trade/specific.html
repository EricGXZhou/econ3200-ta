The Specific Factors Model

The Ricardian model shows that countries gain from trade, but it has only one factor of production: labor.

Workers can move freely across sectors, so adjustment to trade is painless.

The Specific Factors Model adds factors that cannot move across industries in the short run. This allows us to ask:

If trade benefits the economy as a whole, can some people still lose?

1. Model Setup

There are two sectors:

  • Manufacturing \(M\)
  • Agriculture \(A\)

Manufacturing uses capital and labor:

\[ Q_M = 5K^{0.5}L_M^{0.5}. \]

Agriculture uses land and labor:

\[ Q_A = 10T^{0.5}L_A^{0.5}. \]

Labor is mobile across sectors:

\[ L=L_M+L_A. \]

But capital and land are specific factors:

  • \(K\) can only be used in manufacturing;
  • \(T\) can only be used in agriculture.

Suppose

\[ K=100, \qquad T=81. \]

Then

\[ Q_M=50L_M^{0.5}, \]

and

\[ Q_A=90L_A^{0.5}. \]

The marginal products of labor are

\[ \boxed{ MPL_M=25L_M^{-0.5} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ MPL_A=45L_A^{-0.5}. } \]

Because the specific factors are fixed,

\[ L_M\uparrow \Rightarrow MPL_M\downarrow, \]

and

\[ L_A\uparrow \Rightarrow MPL_A\downarrow. \]

This is diminishing marginal product of labor.

More workers have to share the same amount of capital or land.

NoteCRS vs. Diminishing MPL

The production functions have constant returns to scale because increasing both inputs proportionally increases output proportionally.

Diminishing MPL asks a different question: what happens when labor increases while the specific factor is fixed?

So there is no contradiction.

2. Labor Market Equilibrium

A competitive firm hires labor until

\[ \boxed{ W=P_iMPL_i. } \]

Therefore,

\[ W=P_M MPL_M \]

in manufacturing, and

\[ W=P_A MPL_A \]

in agriculture.

Since labor can move between sectors, the wage must be the same in both:

\[ \boxed{ P_M MPL_M=P_A MPL_A. } \]

Substituting the marginal products,

\[ P_M25L_M^{-0.5} = P_A45L_A^{-0.5}. \]

Rearranging,

\[ \frac{P_M}{P_A} = \frac95 \left( \frac{L_M}{L_A} \right)^{0.5}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{L_M}{L_A} = \frac{25}{81} \left( \frac{P_M}{P_A} \right)^2. } \]

This gives the central prediction:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{P_M}{P_A}\uparrow \Rightarrow \frac{L_M}{L_A}\uparrow. } \]

When manufacturing becomes relatively more valuable, workers move from agriculture into manufacturing.

Unlike the Ricardian model, this adjustment is gradual because

\[ L_M\uparrow \Rightarrow MPL_M\downarrow. \]

3. Numerical Example

Suppose total labor is

\[ L=50, \]

and

\[ P_A=1, \qquad P_M=\frac95. \]

Then

\[ \frac{L_M}{L_A} = \frac{25}{81} \left( \frac95 \right)^2 =1. \]

Therefore,

\[ L_M=L_A=25. \]

The equilibrium wage is

\[ W = P_M MPL_M = \frac95 \left[25(25)^{-0.5}\right] =9. \]

The same wage is obtained from agriculture:

\[ W = P_A MPL_A = 1\left[45(25)^{-0.5}\right] =9. \]

4. A Change in Relative Prices

Suppose Home exports manufacturing goods.

Opening to trade raises the relative price of manufacturing:

\[ \frac{P_M}{P_A}\uparrow. \]

For simplicity, keep

\[ P_A=1 \]

and let \(P_M\) double from

\[ \frac95 \]

to

\[ \frac{18}{5}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{L_M}{L_A} = \frac{25}{81} \left( \frac{18}{5} \right)^2 =4. \]

Since

\[ L_M+L_A=50, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ L_M=40, \qquad L_A=10. } \]

Workers move from agriculture into manufacturing.

So

\[ Q_M\uparrow, \qquad Q_A\downarrow. \]

The new wage is

\[ W = P_A MPL_A = 45(10)^{-0.5} \approx14.23. \]

Thus,

\[ W:9\rightarrow14.23. \]

The wage rises, but by less than the price of manufacturing.

Why?

Because

\[ W=P_M MPL_M. \]

When workers enter manufacturing,

\[ L_M\uparrow \Rightarrow MPL_M\downarrow. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta W}{W} < \frac{\Delta P_M}{P_M}. } \]

5. Who Gains and Who Loses?

Mobile Labor

Workers’ wage rises.

Since \(P_A\) is unchanged,

\[ \frac{W}{P_A}\uparrow. \]

Workers can buy more agricultural goods.

But \(P_M\) rises by more than the wage:

\[ \frac{W}{P_M}\downarrow. \]

Workers can buy fewer manufacturing goods.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{The effect on mobile labor is ambiguous.} } \]

Capital Specific to Manufacturing

Capital receives what remains after manufacturing workers are paid:

\[ \text{income of }K = P_MQ_M-WL_M. \]

When \(P_M\) rises:

  • manufacturing expands;
  • more workers enter manufacturing;
  • the return to manufacturing capital rises.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{The specific factor in the expanding sector gains.} } \]

Land Specific to Agriculture

Land receives

\[ \text{income of }T = P_AQ_A-WL_A. \]

When workers leave agriculture:

  • agricultural output falls;
  • the wage rises;
  • the return to land falls.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{The specific factor in the shrinking sector loses.} } \]

Summary

Factor Sector Effect
Capital \(K\) Export sector \(M\) Gains
Land \(T\) Import-competing sector \(A\) Loses
Labor \(L\) Mobile Ambiguous

The main lesson is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Trade can benefit the economy overall while creating winners and losers.} } \]

6. Application: The China Shock

The labels “manufacturing” and “agriculture” are only examples.

Suppose a U.S. industry competes with imports from China.

Cheaper Chinese imports lower the price received by U.S. producers in that industry:

\[ P_I\downarrow. \]

Therefore,

\[ P_I MPL_I\downarrow, \]

so labor demand in the import-competing industry falls.

The Specific Factors Model predicts:

  • employment falls in the import-competing sector;
  • mobile workers move toward other sectors;
  • factors specific to the import-competing sector lose;
  • consumers benefit from lower prices.

So these statements can both be true:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Consumers gain from cheaper imports} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \text{some workers and businesses lose from import competition.} } \]

In reality, workers may also have industry-specific skills or experience. This can make adjustment slower and more costly than the simple model assumes.

7. Practice Problems

Practice 1: Diminishing Marginal Product

Suppose

\[ Q_M=5K^{0.5}L_M^{0.5}. \]

  1. Show that the production function has constant returns to scale.
  2. Derive \(MPL_M\).
  3. Holding \(K\) fixed, what happens to \(MPL_M\) when \(L_M\) rises?
  4. Explain why constant returns to scale and diminishing MPL are not contradictory.

Practice 2: Labor Market Equilibrium

Suppose

\[ Q_M=50L_M^{0.5}, \qquad Q_A=90L_A^{0.5}, \]

with

\[ L=50, \qquad P_A=1, \qquad P_M=\frac95. \]

  1. Find \(L_M/L_A\).
  2. Find \(L_M\) and \(L_A\).
  3. Calculate the equilibrium wage.
  4. Explain why the wage must be equal across sectors.

Practice 3: A Higher Manufacturing Price

Continue from Practice 2.

Suppose

\[ P_M \]

doubles to

\[ \frac{18}{5}, \]

while \(P_A=1\).

  1. Find the new \(L_M/L_A\).
  2. Find the new \(L_M\) and \(L_A\).
  3. Calculate the new wage.
  4. Does the wage rise by more or less than \(P_M\)?
  5. Is mobile labor necessarily better off?
  6. Which specific factor gains and which loses?

Practice 4: The China Shock

A student says:

“Cheaper imports from China benefit U.S. consumers, so U.S. workers cannot be hurt.”

Use the Specific Factors Model to evaluate this statement.

  1. What happens to labor demand in the import-competing sector?
  2. What happens to employment in that sector?
  3. What happens to factors specific to that sector?
  4. Why can consumers gain while some workers and businesses lose?
  5. Does the existence of losers imply that the country as a whole loses from trade?
