Import Tariffs under Perfect Competition

How does an import tariff affect prices, production, consumption, trade, and national welfare?

Why does the answer depend on whether the importing country is small or large?

Model Setup

We study one good traded between two countries:

  • Home imports the good
  • Foreign exports the good to Home
  • Firms and consumers are perfectly competitive
  • There are no transportation costs
  • The analysis is partial equilibrium: we focus on one market
  • Tariff revenue is included in Home national welfare
  • Foreign does not retaliate against Home’s tariff

Let

  • \(P\) = price in Home
  • \(P^*\) = price received by Foreign exporters
  • \(P^W\) = free-trade world price
  • \(t\) = specific tariff per unit imported

Home import demand and Foreign export supply are

\[ M(P)=D(P)-S(P), \qquad X^*(P^*)=S^*(P^*)-D^*(P^*). \]

Under free trade,

\[ P=P^*=P^W, \qquad M(P^W)=X^*(P^W). \]

A specific tariff creates a wedge between the Home and Foreign prices:

\[ P=P^*+t. \]

Home national welfare is

\[ W_H = CS + PS + TR, \qquad TR = tM. \]

1. Domestic Import Demand and Foreign Export Supply

Home Import Demand

At price \(P\), Home consumers demand \(D(P)\) and Home producers supply \(S(P)\). The difference is imported:

\[ M(P)=D(P)-S(P). \]

A lower price raises demand and reduces supply, so the import-demand curve slopes downward.

Suppose

\[ D(P)=\alpha-\beta P, \qquad S(P)=\gamma+\delta P, \]

where \(\beta>0\) and \(\delta>0\). Then

\[ M(P) = (\alpha-\gamma)-(\beta+\delta)P \equiv m_0-m_1P, \]

where

\[ m_0=\alpha-\gamma, \qquad m_1=\beta+\delta>0. \]

The Home no-trade price, \(P^A\), satisfies \(M(P^A)=0\):

\[ P^A=\frac{m_0}{m_1}. \]

At each price, Home imports the horizontal difference between domestic demand and domestic supply. Collecting these differences gives the downward-sloping import-demand curve.

Foreign Export Supply

At price \(P^*\), Foreign producers supply \(S^*(P^*)\) and Foreign consumers demand \(D^*(P^*)\). The difference is exported:

\[ X^*(P^*)=S^*(P^*)-D^*(P^*). \]

A higher Foreign price raises supply and reduces demand, so the export-supply curve slopes upward.

Suppose

\[ D^*(P^*)=\alpha^*-\beta^*P^*, \qquad S^*(P^*)=\gamma^*+\delta^*P^*. \]

Then

\[ X^*(P^*) = (\gamma^*-\alpha^*) + (\beta^*+\delta^*)P^* \equiv x_0+x_1P^*, \]

where

\[ x_0=\gamma^*-\alpha^*, \qquad x_1=\beta^*+\delta^*>0. \]

The Foreign no-trade price, \(P^{A*}\), satisfies \(X^*(P^{A*})=0\):

\[ P^{A*}=-\frac{x_0}{x_1}. \]

Home imports from Foreign when

\[ P^A>P^{A*}. \]

Free-Trade Equilibrium

Under free trade, Home import demand equals Foreign export supply:

\[ m_0-m_1P^W = x_0+x_1P^W. \]

Therefore,

\[ P^W = \frac{m_0-x_0}{m_1+x_1}, \qquad M_1=M(P^W)=X^*(P^W). \]

Foreign exports are the difference between Foreign supply and Foreign demand. The free-trade world price equates Home imports with Foreign exports.

2. Tariff in a Small Country

A small country cannot affect the price received by Foreign exporters. It therefore faces a perfectly elastic Foreign export supply at \(P^W\).

Before the tariff,

\[ P_1=P^W, \qquad M_1=D_1-S_1. \]

After Home imposes a specific tariff \(t\),

\[ P_2=P^W+t, \qquad M_2=D_2-S_2. \]

Because the Home price rises,

\[ D_2<D_1, \qquad S_2>S_1, \qquad M_2<M_1. \]

Using the linear import-demand curve,

\[ M_2 = m_0-m_1(P^W+t) = M_1-m_1t. \]

Therefore,

\[ M_1-M_2=m_1t=(\beta+\delta)t. \]

We assume the tariff is not prohibitive, so \(M_2>0\).

Welfare

For linear demand and supply,

\[ \Delta CS = -\frac{D_1+D_2}{2}t, \qquad \Delta PS = \frac{S_1+S_2}{2}t, \qquad \Delta TR=tM_2. \]

Adding the three components gives

\[ \Delta W_H = -\frac{1}{2}t(M_1-M_2) = -\frac{1}{2}m_1t^2<0. \]

The two parts of the deadweight loss are

\[ \text{Production loss} = \frac{1}{2}\delta t^2, \qquad \text{Consumption loss} = \frac{1}{2}\beta t^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ DWL = \frac{1}{2}(\beta+\delta)t^2 = \frac{1}{2}t(M_1-M_2). \]

Using the areas in the tariff diagram,

\[ \Delta CS=-(a+b+c+d), \qquad \Delta PS=a, \qquad \Delta TR=c, \]

so

\[ \Delta W_H=-(b+d). \]

3. Tariff in a Large Country

A large country imports enough of the good that a reduction in its imports lowers the price received by Foreign exporters.

After the tariff, equilibrium requires

\[ M(P_2)=X^*(P_2^*), \qquad P_2=P_2^*+t. \]

Using the linear curves,

\[ m_0-m_1(P_2^*+t) = x_0+x_1P_2^*. \]

Solving for the two prices gives

\[ P_2^* = P^W-\frac{m_1}{m_1+x_1}t, \qquad P_2 = P^W+\frac{x_1}{m_1+x_1}t. \]

Therefore,

\[ P_2^*<P^W<P_2. \]

The Home price rises by less than the tariff because the Foreign export price falls:

\[ P_2-P^W = \frac{x_1}{m_1+x_1}t, \qquad P^W-P_2^* = \frac{m_1}{m_1+x_1}t. \]

Together,

\[ t = (P_2-P^W)+(P^W-P_2^*). \]

Post-tariff imports are

\[ M_2 = M_1-\frac{m_1x_1}{m_1+x_1}t. \]

We assume the tariff is not prohibitive, so \(M_2>0\).

Welfare

The change in Home consumer and producer surplus is

\[ \Delta CS+\Delta PS = -\frac{M_1+M_2}{2}(P_2-P^W). \]

Because tariff revenue is zero before the tariff,

\[ \Delta TR=tM_2. \]

Using

\[ t=(P_2-P^W)+(P^W-P_2^*), \]

the change in Home welfare becomes

\[ \Delta W_H = (P^W-P_2^*)M_2 - \frac{1}{2}(P_2-P^W)(M_1-M_2). \]

The first term is the terms-of-trade gain:

\[ e=(P^W-P_2^*)M_2. \]

The second term is the domestic production and consumption loss:

\[ b+d = \frac{1}{2}(P_2-P^W)(M_1-M_2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta W_H=e-(b+d). \]

Home gains if \(e>b+d\) and loses if \(e<b+d\).

Comparing Small- and Large-Country Tariffs

In the small-country case, the Foreign price remains at \(P^W\), so the Home price rises by the full tariff. In the large-country case, the tariff lowers the Foreign export price to \(P_2^*\), so the Home price rises by less than \(t\).

Small Country Large Country
Foreign export supply Perfectly elastic Upward sloping
Foreign export price Unchanged Falls
Increase in Home price Equals \(t\) Less than \(t\)
Terms-of-trade gain None \(e>0\)
Home welfare Falls May rise or fall

4. The Optimal Tariff

The optimal tariff is the tariff that maximizes Home national welfare, assuming that Foreign does not retaliate.

Starting from free trade, a small tariff can raise Home welfare when the terms-of-trade gain exceeds the deadweight loss. As the tariff becomes larger, imports fall and the deadweight loss eventually exceeds the terms-of-trade gain. A prohibitive tariff eliminates imports and returns Home to autarky.

Recall that

\[ W_H=CS+PS+TR, \qquad TR=tM. \]

A small change in the Home price changes consumer and producer surplus by

\[ dCS=-D\,dP, \qquad dPS=S\,dP. \]

Therefore,

\[ d(CS+PS) = -(D-S)dP = -M\,dP. \]

The change in tariff revenue is

\[ dTR=d(tM)=M\,dt+t\,dM. \]

Because \(P=P^*+t\), we have \(dP=dP^*+dt\). Thus,

\[ dW_H = -MdP+Mdt+t\,dM = -MdP^*+t\,dM. \]

Since \(P^*\) depends on the quantity imported,

\[ dP^* = \frac{dP^*}{dM}dM. \]

Therefore,

\[ dW_H = \left( t-M\frac{dP^*}{dM} \right)dM. \]

At the optimal tariff,

\[ t^{opt} = M\frac{dP^*}{dM}. \]

Define the Foreign export-supply elasticity as

\[ E_X^* = \frac{dM}{dP^*}\frac{P^*}{M}. \]

Taking the reciprocal,

\[ \frac{dP^*}{dM} = \frac{P^*}{E_X^*M}. \]

Substituting into the optimal-tariff condition gives

\[ t^{opt} = \frac{P^*}{E_X^*}. \]

Let the ad valorem tariff rate be

\[ \tau=\frac{t}{P^*}. \]

The optimal ad valorem tariff is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \tau^{opt} = \frac{1}{E_X^*} }. \]

The elasticity in this formula is evaluated at the optimal tariff equilibrium.

For a small country, Foreign export supply is perfectly elastic:

\[ E_X^*\rightarrow\infty, \qquad \tau^{opt}\rightarrow0. \]

For a large country, a less elastic Foreign export supply implies a higher optimal tariff:

\[ E_X^*\downarrow \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau^{opt}\uparrow. \]

5. Tariff Pass-Through

Section 3 showed that a tariff imposed by a large country can be divided between

  • a higher price paid by Home importers, and
  • a lower price received by Foreign exporters.

Tariff pass-through measures how much of a tariff increase appears in the tariff-inclusive import price paid in Home.

Import Prices with an Ad Valorem Tariff

For this section, we work directly with an ad valorem tariff rate \(\tau\).

Let

  • \(p\) = tariff-inclusive import price in Home
  • \(p^*\) = price received by the Foreign exporter
  • \(\kappa\) = transportation-cost factor

Then

\[ p=(1+\tau)\kappa p^*. \]

Taking logs gives

\[ \ln p = \ln(1+\tau)+\ln\kappa+\ln p^*. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta\ln p = \Delta\ln(1+\tau) + \Delta\ln\kappa + \Delta\ln p^*. \]

If transportation costs do not change, \(\Delta\ln\kappa=0\), so

\[ \Delta\ln p = \Delta\ln(1+\tau) + \Delta\ln p^*. \]

NoteThe log approximation

For a small tariff rate,

\[ \ln(1+\tau)\approx\tau. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta\ln(1+\tau)\approx\Delta\tau, \]

and the price equation can be written as

\[ \Delta\ln p \approx \Delta\tau+\Delta\ln p^*. \]

This is a first-order approximation and is most accurate when tariff rates and tariff changes are small.

For example, when the tariff rises from \(0\) to \(20\%\), the exact change in the tariff wedge is

\[ \ln(1.20)\approx0.182, \]

while the approximation uses

\[ \Delta\tau=0.20. \]

Defining Tariff Pass-Through

Using the approximation above,

\[ \boxed{ \text{pass-through} = \frac{\Delta\ln p}{\Delta\tau} \approx 1+ \frac{\Delta\ln p^*}{\Delta\tau} }. \]

Without the approximation, \(\Delta\tau\) in the denominator is replaced by \(\Delta\ln(1+\tau)\):

\[ \text{exact pass-through} = \frac{\Delta\ln p}{\Delta\ln(1+\tau)}. \]

The value of pass-through tells us how the tariff is divided between Home importers and Foreign exporters.

Pass-through Foreign export price Interpretation
\(1\) Does not change The tariff is fully passed through to the Home import price
Between \(0\) and \(1\) Falls Part of the tariff is absorbed by Foreign exporters
\(0\) Falls enough to offset the tariff The Home import price does not rise

Full pass-through occurs when

\[ \Delta\ln p^*=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{pass-through}=1. \]

Incomplete pass-through occurs when

\[ \Delta\ln p^*<0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{pass-through}<1. \]

This result connects directly to Foreign export-supply elasticity:

  • If Foreign export supply is elastic, Foreign exporters lower their price only slightly, so pass-through is close to one.
  • If Foreign export supply is inelastic, Foreign exporters lower their price more, so pass-through is lower.

Numerical Example

Suppose the tariff rises from zero to \(20\%\):

\[ \Delta\tau=0.20. \]

Suppose the Foreign export price falls by approximately \(10\%\):

\[ \Delta\ln p^*=-0.10. \]

Using the lecture approximation,

\[ \Delta\ln p \approx 0.20-0.10 = 0.10. \]

Therefore,

\[ \text{pass-through} = \frac{0.10}{0.20} = \frac{1}{2}. \]

The tariff-inclusive import price rises by approximately \(10\%\). About one-half of the tariff appears in the Home import price, while the other half is absorbed through a lower Foreign export price.

Theory and Evidence

The small-country model predicts full pass-through because the Foreign export price is fixed:

\[ \Delta\ln p^*=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{pass-through}=1. \]

The standard large-country model instead predicts incomplete pass-through. A tariff reduces Home import demand, causing Foreign exporters to lower their price:

\[ \Delta\ln p^*<0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{pass-through}<1. \]

The evidence from recent U.S. tariffs was therefore surprising:

Episode Pass-through to U.S. import prices Share absorbed by Foreign exporters
Trade War 1.0 Approximately \(100\%\) Approximately \(0\%\)
January–August 2025 \(94\%\) \(6\%\)
November 2025 \(86\%\) \(14\%\)

During Trade War 1.0, multiple studies found nearly full pass-through, implying that Foreign export prices did not fall.

In the first eight months of 2025, a \(10\%\) tariff was associated with only a \(0.6\%\) decline in Foreign export prices. The remaining \(9.4\%\) appeared in higher U.S. import prices:

\[ 10\%\times0.94=9.4\%. \]

By November, a \(10\%\) tariff was associated with a \(1.4\%\) decline in Foreign export prices and an \(8.6\%\) increase in U.S. import prices:

\[ 10\%\times0.86=8.6\%. \]

Thus, pass-through declined somewhat during 2025, but U.S. importers still absorbed most of the tariff.

Practice 1: Domestic Demand Is Not Import Demand

Home demand and supply are

\[ D(P)=100-P, \qquad S(P)=20+P. \]

Foreign demand and supply are

\[ D^*(P^*)=50-P^*, \qquad S^*(P^*)=30+P^*. \]

  1. Derive Home import demand \(M(P)\) and Foreign export supply \(X^*(P^*)\). Find the no-trade price in each country and determine which country imports the good.

  2. Solve for the free-trade world price \(P^W\) and the equilibrium quantity traded \(M_1\).

  3. Suppose Home demand and supply both increase by 10 units at every price:

\[ D'(P)=110-P, \qquad S'(P)=30+P. \]

A student argues:

“Home imports must increase because domestic demand has increased.”

Is the student correct? Derive the new import-demand curve and determine whether the free-trade equilibrium changes.

  1. Return to the original Home demand curve, but suppose only Home supply increases:

\[ D(P)=100-P, \qquad S'(P)=30+P. \]

Solve for the new world price and trade volume. Explain why a change in Home supply can affect the world price even though Foreign demand and supply have not changed.

Practice 2: The Same Tariff in a Small and a Large Country

Home demand and supply are

\[ D(P)=100-P, \qquad S(P)=20+P. \]

The initial free-trade world price is

\[ P^W=25. \]

Home therefore initially imports

\[ M_1=D(P^W)-S(P^W). \]

Suppose Home imposes a specific tariff

\[ t=10. \]

  1. First suppose Home is a small country. Find the tariff-inclusive Home price, domestic consumption, domestic production, and imports after the tariff.

  2. For the small-country case, calculate

\[ \Delta CS, \qquad \Delta PS, \qquad TR, \qquad \Delta W_H. \]

Which changes are transfers within Home, and which represent a true efficiency loss?

  1. Now suppose Home is a large country and faces Foreign export supply

\[ X^*(P^*)=-20+2P^*. \]

Solve for the post-tariff Foreign export price \(P_2^*\), the Home price \(P_2=P_2^*+t\), and imports \(M_2\).

  1. In the large-country case, tariff revenue can be decomposed as

Calculate \(e\), \(b+d\), and \(\Delta W_H\). Explain why area \(c\) is not a terms-of-trade gain, while area \(e\) is.

Practice 5: Pass-Through, Transport Costs, and the Log Approximation

An ad valorem tariff rises from \(0\%\) to \(20\%\), so

\[ \Delta\tau=0.20. \]

At the same time,

\[ \Delta\ln\kappa=0.04, \qquad \Delta\ln p^*=-0.08, \]

where \(\kappa\) is the transportation-cost factor and \(p^*\) is the Foreign export price.

You may use

\[ \ln(1.20)\approx0.182. \]

  1. Using the lecture approximation,

\[ \Delta\ln p \approx \Delta\tau+\Delta\ln\kappa+\Delta\ln p^*, \]

calculate the approximate change in the tariff-inclusive Home import price.

  1. A student divides the observed import-price change by \(0.20\) and reports that number as tariff pass-through.

Explain why this calculation is incorrect when transportation costs have changed. After removing the transport-cost change, calculate the approximate tariff pass-through and the share of the tariff absorbed by Foreign exporters.

  1. Now use the exact decomposition

\[ \Delta\ln p = \Delta\ln(1+\tau) + \Delta\ln\kappa + \Delta\ln p^* \]

to calculate the exact change in the import price and the exact tariff pass-through after controlling for transportation costs. Why do the exact and approximate answers differ?

  1. Suppose an empirical study estimates a pass-through rate of \(0.94\) into U.S. import prices. A student concludes:

Evaluate both parts of this conclusion. Distinguish between country size, import prices at the border, and final retail prices.
